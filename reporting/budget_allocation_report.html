Ad budget allocation · $500,000 per period · simulated risk analysis

Where to put the budget, and what could go wrong

We tested 971 different ways of splitting a $500,000 budget across 5 advertising channels, simulating 10,000 possible outcomes for each under four market conditions. The budget carries no time period — whether that is a month or a year changes whether these figures are plausible at all; see limitations. This is what the results support — and, just as importantly, what they do not.

How to read this

We treat each advertising channel the way an investor treats an asset in a portfolio. A channel has a return (revenue per dollar spent) and a risk (how much that return swings). Channels also move together — when conversion rates dip, they tend to dip across several channels at once — so spreading the budget reduces risk by less than you might assume.

Rather than predict one number, we simulate 10,000 possible futures for every candidate split. That produces a distribution, which lets us talk about the bad cases directly:

  • Expected revenue — the average across all 10,000 simulated outcomes.
  • Volatility — how widely those outcomes spread. Lower means more predictable.
  • Worst-case floor (VaR-95) — you beat this number in 95% of simulations. A plain-language version: "19 times out of 20, revenue lands above this."
  • Deep-tail average (CVaR-95) — the average of the worst 5% of outcomes. This is what a bad year actually looks like, not just its boundary.

An allocation is efficient if nothing else beats it on both return and risk at once. Plot all the efficient ones and you get a frontier: the menu of sensible trade-offs.

Two frontiers, and only one of them is a real curve

Judged on return vs volatility, 107–123 of 971 allocations are efficient (11.0–12.7%). That is a genuine menu of choices.

Judged on the worst-case floor, only 6–9 are efficient; on the deep-tail average, 8–11. Those are handfuls, not curves.

This is not a discovery. The number of efficient points in any two-way trade-off grows roughly with the logarithm of how many candidates you test — so a short list is the expected result at this sample size, not evidence that return and downside risk are secretly the same thing. We tested that specific explanation and it did not hold. Read the worst-case frontiers as a few defensible options, and use the volatility frontier when you want to see the shape of the trade-off.

What the caveats mean

Ordering unresolved — this pick and its neighbour on the frontier are close enough together that the simulation cannot tell them apart. The choice between them is not supported by the data; either is defensible. 11 of 36 recommendations across all scenarios carry this.

Priced at floor — the allocation funds at least one channel below any spend level the historical data covers, so its cost-per-click was priced at the lowest observed level rather than extrapolated below it. The number is bounded and defensible, but it sits at the edge of the evidence. 16 of 36 recommendations carry this, along with 50 of 519 frontier points and 692 of 3,884 tested allocations. No recommendation carries both flags at once.

The lowest-volatility option is not the safest one

"Conservative" here means least variable, not least likely to lose money. Under a platform change it returns 0.83× — $413,740 on $500,000 of spend, a real loss — and it loses money in a recession too. The aggressive pick returns 1.60× in that same bad scenario.

If the goal is to avoid losing money rather than to avoid surprises, the low-volatility allocation is the wrong instrument.

Be precise about why, because the model is simpler than this table looks. Each condition applies a near-uniform multiplier to every allocation — across all 971 candidates the recession factor ranges from 0.8192 to 0.8297 (a 0.0105 spread) — so these columns are essentially the normal one times three constants. No allocation is specifically fragile to a downturn in this model. The conservative pick loses money because its base-case return is barely above break-even at 1.09×, not because steadiness makes it brittle. Anything below roughly 1.32× in normal conditions loses money under a platform change; 103 of 971 candidates are.

Revenue-seeking weight and risk-reducing weight are not the same. paid_search takes 47% of the aggressive budget. paid_search supplies the largest expected-revenue contribution at 53.8% of the total. paid_search supplies the largest component of portfolio volatility at 70.8%, while video supplies the smallest at 0.1%.

How to read component volatility. For each channel it is the covariance of that channel's simulated revenue with total simulated revenue, divided by total volatility. This Euler decomposition adds back to portfolio volatility exactly. A negative component is a diversification benefit — it dampens the total — rather than impossible negative risk. It is a model-based attribution, not proof that changing one channel alone would cause the same change in revenue or risk.

The attribution reuses the original allocation, scenario, seed and 10,000 paths from Phase 5 source Workflow run 322037088744573; its channel sums are reconciled to the stored gold mean and standard deviation. Method: euler_std_v1: cov(channel,total)/std(total); reconciled_to_gold.

Limitations

These are not hedges. Each one changes how much weight the recommendations can carry.

1. Diminishing returns are an assumption, and the data does not confirm them

The model assumes each channel gets more expensive as you spend more, at a rate set per channel. Those rates were chosen by judgement. Their ordering is defensible — it follows each channel's inventory size, and search having the least room to grow matches how search advertising works.

But a later check regressed real cost-per-click against real spend in the historical data and did not reproduce those rates: the measured relationships came out roughly uniform across channels, with paid search — the channel assigned the steepest curve — measuring among the flattest. Being fair to the assumption: within a channel, daily spend only ever varied about three- to fourfold, and the entire observed range sits below 7% of the spend level the curve is anchored at. The measured relationships are statistically solid where they were measured — they were simply never measured anywhere near the budgets being recommended. So the historical record cannot confirm the assumed rates, and cannot fully refute them either.

What this means for the recommendations: the ranking of allocations depends on these rates. Change them and the winner moves. Treat the picks as "best under a stated assumption," not as measured fact.

What would actually settle it: a spend-variation test — deliberately running channels at materially different budget levels and measuring how cost-per-click responds — or a holdout test, switching a channel off in some regions and leaving it on in others, to measure what it genuinely adds. Either produces the spend variation the historical record lacks.

2. Some recommendations are not fully resolved

11 of 36 recommendations are flagged ordering unresolved: the pick is statistically indistinguishable from its neighbour on the frontier. Simulation noise is as large as the gap. Do not read a ranking into those.

16 of 36 are flagged priced at floor: at least one channel is funded below anything in the historical record, so its cost was held at the lowest observed level instead of extrapolated downward. This bounds the estimate rather than fixing it — the optimizer can still propose a channel funded at a token amount, and here the smallest funded channel is $1,128. The attribution reports the simulated contribution under the stated model, but that does not create empirical evidence below the historical spend floor.

3. The distributed computing was proven, but was not necessary

The pipeline runs on Spark and is orchestrated as a persisted, parameterised workflow that was triggered and verified end to end. It runs on demand rather than on a schedule — no cron trigger is attached. Honestly reported: at this project's scale the distributed computing did not need to be there. The full simulation grid runs in about seven seconds on a single machine. Spark earns its keep in the 971-candidate optimization sweep, but the earlier stages demonstrate the mechanism rather than require it. A production version at ten or a hundred times this scale would need it; this one does not.

4. Scenario severities are judgement, and correlation is held fixed

The four market conditions are stated assumptions about how conversion rates, click costs and order values move together — not forecasts, and not fitted from historical downturns. Separately, the model holds cross-channel correlation constant across scenarios. In a real downturn correlations typically rise, which means the recession case here understates tail risk rather than overstating it.
